<h2>A gas exporter with no way out</h2>

<p>Qatar is the second-largest exporter of liquefied natural gas in the world, behind only the United States. In 2025, the country shipped approximately 107 billion cubic meters of LNG, which is about 21 percent of total global LNG trade. Nearly every single cubic meter of that gas left Qatar through the Strait of Hormuz. There is no other way out. Unlike crude oil, which Saudi Arabia can partially redirect through its East-West Pipeline to Red Sea terminals, LNG has no pipeline alternative. You cannot pipe liquefied natural gas over land. It has to travel by ship. And the ships have to go through the strait.</p>

<p>This simple geographic fact is now the single biggest vulnerability in global energy security. The New York Times reported on May 20 that "In Qatar, Energy Sector Damage Is Severe, and the Way Back Will Be Hard." The Times was describing the cascading effects of the Hormuz closure on Qatar's LNG operations, and the description was accurate. Qatar's LNG export capacity is not merely constrained. It is effectively shut down. The tankers are not moving.</p>

<p>I want to be precise about what "effectively shut down" means. It does not mean zero LNG is leaving Qatar. A handful of vessels have transited the strait under naval escort as part of Operation Project Freedom. But the volume is a fraction of normal. In April, Qatar exported roughly 4.2 million metric tons of LNG, according to data from Kpler, a commodity analytics firm. In a normal month, Qatar exports about 8.5 to 9 million metric tons. That is a decline of more than 50 percent, and the May numbers are likely to be worse.</p>

<h2>The geography problem</h2>

<p>Let me explain why Qatar is in this position, because it is worth understanding the geography in detail. Qatar sits on a peninsula that juts out from the eastern coast of Saudi Arabia into the Persian Gulf. Its LNG export terminals, Ras Laffan Industrial City, are on the northeast coast of the Qatari peninsula, roughly 180 nautical miles from the Strait of Hormuz. Every LNG carrier that leaves Ras Laffan must sail north through the Persian Gulf and transit the strait to reach the open waters of the Gulf of Oman and the Arabian Sea.</p>

<p>There is no pipeline alternative. I keep seeing suggestions in commentary that Qatar could pipe gas overland through Saudi Arabia. This is theoretically possible for natural gas in its gaseous state, and in fact the Dolphin Pipeline already carries Qatari gas to the UAE and Oman. But the Dolphin Pipeline has a capacity of about 3.2 billion cubic meters per year, which is roughly 3 percent of Qatar's LNG export volume. You cannot scale pipeline capacity quickly enough to matter. Building a new pipeline across Saudi Arabia to the Red Sea would take five to seven years and require Saudi cooperation that is not currently on offer. The two countries have a historically complicated relationship, and while they restored diplomatic ties in 2023 after the GCC rift, there is no appetite in Riyadh for a massive infrastructure project that primarily benefits Doha.</p>

<p>LNG is different from crude oil in another way that compounds the problem. Oil can be stored in tanks and on floating vessels for extended periods. LNG boils off. Even the best-maintained LNG carrier loses roughly 0.1 to 0.15 percent of its cargo per day to boil-off. After three months of sitting at anchor, a carrier loaded with 170,000 cubic meters of LNG will have lost roughly 15,000 cubic meters of cargo. That is about $4.5 million worth of gas at current prices, just evaporated into the atmosphere.</p>

<h2>The stranded fleet</h2>

<p>As of May 20, there are 23 LNG carriers at anchor near Ras Laffan, waiting to either load or depart. QatarEnergy, the state-owned company that operates Qatar's LNG infrastructure, has declined to provide specific numbers. But vessel tracking data from MarineTraffic and Kpler shows the buildup clearly. In January, before the crisis, the average wait time at Ras Laffan was less than 24 hours. Now it is measured in weeks.</p>

<p>Some of these carriers are fully loaded and cannot move because their owners or charterers have determined that a transit through the strait is too risky. The war risk insurance premium for an LNG carrier transiting Hormuz has increased from roughly 0.025 percent of hull value to approximately 1.5 percent, according to brokers at Lloyd's. For a modern LNG carrier valued at $220 million, that means the insurance cost for a single transit has gone from about $55,000 to $3.3 million. At that price, it is cheaper to sit at anchor and eat the boil-off losses.</p>

<p>Other carriers are empty, having arrived to load cargo that may or may not be able to leave. QatarEnergy has continued to produce LNG at its Ras Laffan trains, filling onshore storage tanks. But the storage capacity is finite. Qatar's LNG storage tanks at Ras Laffan can hold roughly 2.8 million cubic meters of LNG. With exports running at roughly half of normal, the tanks are filling up. If they reach capacity, QatarEnergy will have to curtail production from the liquefaction trains, which is not a simple on-off switch. Shutting down and restarting an LNG train can take weeks and costs tens of millions of dollars.</p>

<h2>Who depends on Qatari gas</h2>

<p>The impact of the Qatari LNG disruption falls hardest on the countries that have structured their energy supply around long-term contracts with QatarEnergy. Japan is the single largest buyer, importing approximately 29 billion cubic meters per year from Qatar under contracts that run through 2032 and beyond. South Korea buys about 15 billion cubic meters. India imports roughly 21 billion cubic meters. China takes about 18 billion cubic meters. The UK buys approximately 12 billion cubic meters under a long-term contract signed in 2022.</p>

<p>These are not spot market purchases. They are long-term supply agreements, typically 20 to 25 years in duration, with take-or-pay clauses. This means that the buyer is contractually obligated to pay for the gas whether they receive it or not. The force majeure provisions in most of these contracts are being invoked by QatarEnergy, which relieves both parties of their obligations during the crisis. But force majeure does not replace the gas. It just makes the legal situation clear. The gas is still not flowing.</p>

<p>Japan's Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry has been scrambling to find replacement LNG cargoes from Australia, the United States, and Mozambique. Australia, the world's largest LNG exporter, has some spare capacity, but not much. The Australian LNG industry has been running at near-full capacity for the past two years, and the new Scarborough and Pluto Train 2 projects, operated by Woodside Energy, are not due to come online until late 2027. METI has also approached the US Department of Energy about expedited export approvals for additional LNG cargoes to Japan. The DOE has granted some authorizations, but US LNG export terminals are also running at or near capacity.</p>

<h2>Europe's second energy crisis</h2>

<p>For Europe, the Qatari LNG disruption is a painful echo of the 2022 Russian gas crisis. After Russia cut pipeline gas exports to Europe following the invasion of Ukraine, European countries spent two years building out LNG import infrastructure, signing new supply contracts, and restructuring their energy systems to reduce dependence on Russian gas. By early 2026, Europe had largely succeeded. Pipeline gas from Russia was down to roughly 15 percent of pre-2022 levels, and LNG imports had filled the gap.</p>

<p>But a significant portion of that replacement LNG came from Qatar. The UK's long-term contract with QatarEnergy, signed in 2022, was specifically intended to reduce British dependence on Russian gas. Italy signed a similar deal in 2023. Germany, which built two new FSRUs, or floating storage and regasification units, in record time after the Russian cutoff, was counting on Qatari LNG for roughly 20 percent of its import needs.</p>

<p>Now that gas is not arriving. European LNG import terminals are running at maximum capacity, as the Financial Times reported on May 25, but they are processing cargoes from other sources. The TTF Dutch gas benchmark, Europe's reference price, rose to EUR 48 per megawatt-hour in mid-May, up from EUR 28 in January. That is high but still well below the EUR 340 peak reached in August 2022. The difference this time is that Europe has less room to maneuver. The low-hanging fruit of demand reduction, switching from gas to coal, increasing renewable output, has already been picked during the Russia crisis.</p>

<h2>The Asian scramble</h2>

<p>Asia is where the real pain is concentrated. Japan, South Korea, and China together account for roughly 60 percent of global LNG imports. All three were major buyers of Qatari LNG. All three are now competing for the same limited alternative supplies.</p>

<p>India is in a particularly difficult position. Unlike Japan and South Korea, India does not have significant strategic gas reserves. The country's LNG import infrastructure is concentrated at four terminals: Dahej and Hazira in Gujarat, Dabhol in Maharashtra, and Ennore in Tamil Nadu. Their combined regasification capacity is roughly 42 million metric tons per year, but utilization was already above 85 percent before the crisis. There is limited headroom to absorb additional cargoes even if India could find them.</p>

<p>Petronet LNG, India's largest LNG importer, has been buying spot cargoes from the US and Australia at prices 60 to 80 percent above its long-term contract rates with Qatar. The company's managing director told analysts on a May 19 earnings call that the spot market for LNG is "extremely tight and likely to remain so through the remainder of the year." India's fertilizer industry, which depends on gas as a feedstock, has been forced to cut production. Fertilizer prices in India have risen 35 percent since February, which will eventually translate into higher food costs for the country's 1.4 billion people.</p>

<h2>Why there are no alternatives</h2>

<p>I keep coming back to this question because it is the one that frustrates me the most. The world built its LNG supply chain around the assumption that the Strait of Hormuz would remain open. That assumption was reasonable for decades. Qatar invested $80 billion in its LNG infrastructure on that basis. Japan and Korea signed 25-year contracts on that basis. Europe replaced Russian pipeline gas with Qatari LNG on that basis.</p>

<p>There is no quick alternative. New LNG export projects take five to eight years from final investment decision to first production. The US has several projects under construction, but the earliest, NextDecade's Rio Grande Train 4, is not expected online until late 2028. QatarEnergy itself is building the North Field Expansion project, which will add 48 million metric tons per year of capacity by 2028, but that gas will still need to transit Hormuz. Mozambique's LNG projects, operated by TotalEnergies and ExxonMobil, have been delayed by security problems and are at least two years behind schedule. Canada's LNG Canada project, the country's first major LNG export terminal, is scheduled to start production in mid-2026 but will ship only about 14 million metric tons per year, barely denting the gap left by the Qatari disruption.</p>

<p>The hard truth is that the global LNG market is capacity-constrained in a way that the crude oil market is not. Oil has pipelines, rail, trucks, and strategic reserves. LNG has ships and regasification terminals. When the ships cannot sail and the terminals are full, the system breaks. We are watching it break in real time.</p>

<h2>What Qatar is doing</h2>

<p>Qatar is not sitting idle. The government has engaged in intense diplomatic activity, hosting mediation talks between Iran and the Gulf states and pressing for a de-escalation framework that would allow LNG carriers to transit the strait safely. Qatar's Emir, Sheikh Tamim bin Hamad Al Thani, visited Tehran on May 10 for talks with Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian. The details of that meeting have not been made public, but a Qatari official told Reuters that the emir had proposed a "humanitarian corridor" for LNG traffic through the strait, similar to the grain corridor that operated in the Black Sea during the Russia-Ukraine conflict.</p>

<p>Iran has not formally rejected the proposal, but no concrete progress has been announced. The IRGC, which controls security in the strait, has its own agenda and is not necessarily aligned with the civilian government in Tehran on this issue. The IRGC's published "safe transit" rules for commercial vessels, announced on May 19, include mandatory inspection stops and transit fees that are commercially unworkable for LNG carriers. An inspection stop would require the LNG carrier to anchor for 24 to 48 hours, during which time the cargo continues to boil off and the vessel remains exposed to potential attack.</p>

<p>QatarEnergy has also invoked force majeure on its long-term supply contracts, as I mentioned earlier. This protects the company from breach-of-contract claims, but it does not protect Qatar's reputation as a reliable supplier. Japan and South Korea have already signaled that they will diversify their LNG supply sources more aggressively once the crisis ends, regardless of whether Qatar is ultimately responsible for the disruption. The commercial damage will outlast the physical damage.</p>

<p>The New York Times got it right. The way back will be hard. Even after the strait reopens, Qatar will face a market that has learned, the hard way, that depending on a single geographic chokepoint for 21 percent of the world's LNG is a vulnerability that cannot be ignored. The contracts will be rewritten. The supply chains will be restructured. And Qatar, through no fault of its own, will pay the price for a geography it cannot change.</p>
