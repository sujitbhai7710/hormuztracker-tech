<h2>The mission</h2>

<p>On May 4, the United States Navy launched Operation Project Freedom. The concept is simple on paper: US warships escort commercial vessels through the Strait of Hormuz to protect them from Iranian attack. In practice, it is one of the most dangerous naval missions the United States has undertaken in decades. The strait is 21 nautical miles wide at its narrowest point. The navigation channel for large vessels is roughly six miles across. Iranian fast attack craft operate from bases on Qeshm Island and Abu Musa that are within visual range of the shipping lanes. The IRGC's coastal missile batteries on the Iranian mainland can reach any point in the strait within 90 seconds of launch.</p>

<p>I spent twenty-six years in the Navy, including two tours commanding a guided missile destroyer in the Fifth Fleet area of operations. I have transited the Strait of Hormuz more times than I can count. Every single transit was tense. The IRGC Navy, or IRGCN, has a habit of sending small boats to approach US warships at high speed, cutting across the bow, and training weapons in our direction. They do this during peacetime. Doing it during a crisis, with both sides on a hair trigger, is a recipe for disaster.</p>

<p>Operation Project Freedom is asking sailors to do something that has no recent precedent. The closest comparison is the 1987-88 Operation Earnest Will, when the US Navy reflagged Kuwaiti tankers and escorted them through the Persian Gulf during the Iran-Iraq War. That operation worked, mostly, but it also resulted in the USS Stark being hit by an Iraqi Exocet missile, the USS Samuel B. Roberts hitting an Iranian mine, and the US Navy engaging Iranian platforms in Operation Praying Mantis. Earnest Will was not clean. Project Freedom will not be either.</p>

<h2>How the convoy system works</h2>

<p>The escort formation, according to multiple sources within Fifth Fleet who spoke on condition of anonymity because operational details are classified, follows a fairly standard convoy pattern. Two Arleigh Burke-class guided missile destroyers serve as the primary escort vessels, one leading the convoy and one bringing up the rear. Between them, commercial vessels transit in a single-file column with roughly 1,500 yards of separation between ships. Patrol craft operate on the flanks, watching for fast boat approaches. An MH-60R Seahawk helicopter from the destroyer's air detachment provides overhead surveillance.</p>

<p>A typical convoy consists of three to five commercial vessels. The Navy has been running one convoy roughly every 36 hours, though the schedule varies based on the availability of ships and the assessed threat level on any given day. Each transit through the strait takes approximately four to six hours depending on traffic density and weather conditions.</p>

<p>The destroyers are equipped with the Aegis combat system, which can track hundreds of surface and air contacts simultaneously. Their Standard Missile series, or SM-2 and SM-6, can engage incoming anti-ship cruise missiles at ranges exceeding 100 miles. The Phalanx Close-In Weapon System, or CIWS, provides a last-ditch defense against anything that gets through. These are capable ships. But the strait geography negates many of their advantages. The Iranian C-802 and Qader anti-ship cruise missiles have a flight time of roughly 30 seconds from launch to impact when fired from coastal positions near Bandar Abbas. That is barely enough time for the Aegis system to detect, classify, and engage, even assuming perfect performance.</p>

<h2>Three warships targeted</h2>

<p>Since the operation began, Iran has targeted three US warships transiting the strait. The first incident occurred on May 8, when the USS Carney, an Arleigh Burke-class destroyer leading a convoy of four commercial vessels, detected an incoming radar signature consistent with an anti-ship missile. The Carney's Aegis system engaged and destroyed the incoming threat. The Pentagon confirmed the engagement in a press statement the following day, describing it as a "single anti-ship cruise missile fired from Iranian territory." No US personnel were injured, and the convoy continued its transit.</p>

<p>The second incident came on May 14. The USS Laboon, another destroyer, was transiting the strait with a convoy of three tankers when IRGCN fast attack craft made a high-speed approach. The Laboon fired warning shots and maneuvered to place itself between the approaching boats and the commercial vessels. The IRGCN craft broke off after approximately seven minutes. The Laboon's commanding officer reported that the Iranian boats came within 500 yards, well inside the range at which they could deploy swarming tactics or fire rocket-propelled grenades.</p>

<p>The third incident was the most serious. On May 19, the USS Mason was transiting the strait when it came under simultaneous fire from two directions. The Mason's crew engaged multiple incoming threats and sustained no damage, but the engagement lasted nearly twelve minutes, according to a Defense Department official who spoke on condition of anonymity. The Mason was escorting two LNG carriers at the time. Both commercial vessels were unharmed and completed their transit.</p>

<p>CNN reported on May 21 that "fresh attacks in the Gulf raised doubts about the durability" of the escort mission. That assessment, attributed to two unnamed Defense Department officials, reflects a growing concern within the military that the current force allocation is insufficient for the threat environment.</p>

<h2>The math does not look good</h2>

<p>Here is the problem in plain numbers. The US Fifth Fleet currently has four Arleigh Burke-class destroyers, two Ticonderoga-class cruisers, and a handful of patrol craft available for escort duty in the Persian Gulf. Each destroyer can sustain roughly one convoy escort every 48 hours before it needs to refuel, resupply, and give its crew rest. With four destroyers rotating through the mission, that means roughly two convoys per day maximum.</p>

<p>Before the Hormuz crisis, approximately 17 million barrels of oil per day transited the strait, carried on roughly 30 to 35 large tankers. At two convoys per day with four to five ships each, Operation Project Freedom can escort 8 to 10 commercial vessels daily. That covers roughly 25 to 30 percent of pre-crisis traffic. The rest either stays put, takes its chances without an escort, or reroutes around the Cape of Good Hope.</p>

<p>The IRGCN operates an estimated 1,500 fast attack craft and small boats in the Persian Gulf and Strait of Hormuz area. Many of these are Boghammar-type speedboats armed with heavy machine guns, rocket-propelled grenades, and in some cases, man-portable anti-ship missiles. Their swarm tactics are designed to overwhelm a warship's defensive systems through sheer numbers. A single destroyer can probably handle four to six simultaneous fast boat contacts. More than that, and the math gets ugly fast.</p>

<h2>What happens if a US ship gets hit</h2>

<p>This is the question nobody in the Pentagon wants to talk about publicly, but everyone is thinking about it privately. If an Iranian anti-ship missile connects with a US destroyer, or if a swarm attack overwhelms the CIWS and an RPG or small missile strikes a commercial vessel under escort, the United States faces an immediate escalation dilemma.</p>

<p>There are two probable responses, and neither is good. Option one is a proportional military strike against the specific Iranian assets that conducted the attack: the missile battery, the boat base, the command post that ordered it. This is what the US did after the 1987 attack on the USS Stark (though that was an Iraqi missile) and after the 1988 mining of the USS Samuel B. Roberts, which led to Operation Praying Mantis, the largest surface naval engagement since World War II. A proportional response restores deterrence, in theory, but it also escalates the conflict and risks further Iranian retaliation against shipping.</p>

<p>Option two is a broader military campaign to degrade Iranian naval and missile capabilities in the Gulf. This is what some hawks in Congress and within the administration have been advocating. It would involve strikes on IRGCN bases on Qeshm Island, the naval facility at Bandar Abbas, and the coastal missile sites along the Strait of Hormuz. The military can do this. The Air Force and Navy have the munitions and the targeting data. But Iran has made clear that such strikes would be met with a full-scale campaign against Gulf shipping and potentially against US bases in the region. The USS Bataan amphibious ready group, operating in the Gulf of Oman, would be within range of Iranian ballistic missiles.</p>

<p>I keep thinking about a conversation I had last week with a currently serving destroyer captain who I have known for fifteen years. He told me, and I am paraphrasing here, that every time he takes a convoy through the strait, he is acutely aware that a single miscalculation by a 22-year-old Iranian boat commander could start a war. "I trust my crew," he said. "I do not trust theirs."</p>

<h2>The risk to commercial crews</h2>

<p>The Navy's escort provides a layer of protection, but it also changes the calculus for Iran. A commercial vessel transiting alone might be harassed or seized. A commercial vessel under US Navy escort is, from Iran's perspective, a legitimate target in an armed confrontation with the United States. The escort does not eliminate the risk. It transforms it from a seizure risk into a combat risk.</p>

<p>Commercial mariners understand this. The International Transport Workers' Federation, or ITF, has reported that seafarers are increasingly refusing to sign on for voyages through the Persian Gulf. A survey conducted by the ITF in early May found that 68 percent of the 2,400 seafarers polled said they would not transit the Strait of Hormuz even with a naval escort. The fear is real and rational. A missile does not care whether you are military or civilian. If it hits your ship, the water is just as cold.</p>

<p>Shipping companies are offering hazard pay, typically a 50 to 100 percent bonus on top of regular wages, for Gulf transits. This has helped crew some vessels, but it has not solved the manpower shortage. Several Greek and Philippine crewing agencies have told me they simply cannot find enough willing sailors at any price.</p>

<h2>The operation continues, for now</h2>

<p>As of this writing, Operation Project Freedom is still active. The Navy ran three convoys in the past 48 hours. Nine commercial vessels transited safely. But the margin is thin. One successful Iranian attack on a US warship would force a strategic decision that the administration has so far managed to avoid making. And the longer the operation continues without a diplomatic resolution, the more likely that attack becomes.</p>

<p>The Pentagon has requested additional assets for Fifth Fleet, including a third destroyer and extended patrol craft deployments. The USS Eisenhower Carrier Strike Group, currently in the Arabian Sea, could provide additional air cover and surface combatants if directed. But carrier operations in the confined waters of the Persian Gulf carry their own risks. The USS Nimitz learned this lesson in 1988, when it operated in the Gulf during Praying Mantis and found the shallow, congested waters less than ideal for carrier aviation.</p>

<p>For the sailors on those destroyers, the mission is straightforward even if the politics are not: get the ships through safely and come home. Whether Washington can give them a strategy that matches their courage is an open question.</p>
