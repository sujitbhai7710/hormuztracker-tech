<h2>The number that stopped me</h2>

<p>The Guardian published a figure last week that I have been unable to shake. The Middle East crisis, they reported, could cost the world economy $1 trillion. One trillion dollars. I read the number, then read it again, then pulled up the IMF's latest World Economic Outlook supplement to see if it tracked. The IMF had revised global GDP downward by 0.8 percentage points for 2026. Global GDP in 2025 was roughly $110 trillion. Eight-tenths of one percent of $110 trillion is $880 billion. Close enough to a trillion to make the Guardian's number credible, if slightly on the high side.</p>

<p>But aggregate numbers like that are almost meaningless on their own. A trillion dollars spread across the global economy works out to about $125 per person on Earth. That average hides the reality: some countries are barely feeling the impact, while others are facing genuine economic collapse. The distribution of pain is wildly, almost absurdly, uneven.</p>

<p>The global oil market has lost roughly 8 percent of its supply since the Hormuz crisis began. The IEA confirmed this in its May monthly report, noting that the combination of the strait's effective closure to unescorted traffic, the Iranian seizure of vessels, and the withdrawal of insurance coverage for Gulf transits has removed approximately 8.4 million barrels per day from a market that was previously moving about 102 million barrels per day. The IEA expects this disruption to extend into 2027.</p>

<h2>Who pays and who does not</h2>

<p>The World Economic Forum outlined six broad categories of national response to the crisis: strategic reserve drawdowns, demand restraint programs, fuel subsidies, alternative supply sourcing, diplomatic pressure for de-escalation, and direct financial assistance to affected populations. Every major economy is using some combination of these. The difference is that wealthy countries can afford all six. Poor countries can afford maybe one, and it is usually the wrong one.</p>

<p>Let me walk through the country-by-country impact, because the disparities are staggering.</p>

<h2>Pakistan</h2>

<p>Pakistan is the hardest-hit major economy. The country imports roughly 80 percent of its petroleum needs, and a significant share comes from Gulf suppliers via Hormuz. The State Bank of Pakistan reported in early May that the current account deficit had widened to $2.8 billion for the month of April alone, up from $1.1 billion in March, almost entirely due to higher oil import costs. The Pakistani rupee has lost 12 percent against the dollar since the crisis began. Inflation hit 34 percent year-over-year in May, with fuel prices up 68 percent from January levels.</p>

<p>The government has responded with fuel subsidies, which is the most politically expedient option but also the most fiscally destructive. The subsidy bill for April and May combined is estimated at $3.2 billion, money that Pakistan simply does not have. The IMF, which had been negotiating a $3 billion standby arrangement with Islamabad, has reportedly paused those talks because the fuel subsidies violate the fiscal targets agreed upon in the preliminary framework. A Pakistani finance ministry official, speaking on condition of anonymity because negotiations are ongoing, told me that the country may need to choose between keeping the lights on and meeting its debt obligations. That is not a figure of speech. Pakistan has $4.5 billion in external debt payments due in the second half of 2026.</p>

<h2>India</h2>

<p>India is the world's third-largest oil importer, bringing in roughly 5 million barrels per day. About 60 percent of that normally transits the Strait of Hormuz. The impact on India has been severe but manageable, thanks to the country's large foreign exchange reserves and the Reserve Bank of India's willingness to intervene in currency markets. The rupee is down about 6 percent against the dollar since February, which is painful but not catastrophic.</p>

<p>The real damage is at the consumer level. Diesel prices in India have risen by roughly 40 percent since January. Diesel fuels India's trucks, its trains, its agricultural pumps, and its backup generators. When diesel gets expensive, everything gets expensive. The Wholesale Price Index rose 14.2 percent year-over-year in April, the highest reading since 2013. The government has cut excise duties on fuel twice, sacrificing roughly $18 billion in annual revenue, and has asked state-owned refiners to absorb losses rather than pass on the full cost increase to consumers. Indian Oil Corporation reported a net loss of $1.3 billion in the quarter ending March 31, the first quarterly loss for the company in over a decade.</p>

<h2>Bangladesh and Sri Lanka</h2>

<p>Bangladesh imports virtually all of its petroleum. The Bangladesh Petroleum Corporation has been forced to ration diesel and kerosene since late April. Power outages in Dhaka now last six to eight hours per day, up from two to three hours before the crisis. The garment industry, which accounts for 85 percent of Bangladesh's exports and employs 4 million workers, has been hammered by both higher energy costs and higher shipping costs. The BGMEA, the garment manufacturers' association, estimates that production costs have risen 22 percent since February while order volumes have declined 15 percent as Western buyers shift orders to competitors in Vietnam and Indonesia.</p>

<p>Sri Lanka is in even worse shape. The country barely emerged from its 2022 debt crisis and is still under an IMF program with strict fiscal conditions. Higher oil import costs have pushed the trade deficit to dangerous levels again. The Sri Lankan central bank has raised interest rates by 350 basis points since March, which is crushing what was a fragile economic recovery. Construction activity has essentially stopped. The tourism sector, which had been the main engine of growth, is now struggling as well: fuel shortages have made internal transport unreliable, and several international airlines have reduced Colombo service because of higher jet fuel costs.</p>

<h2>Japan and South Korea</h2>

<p>Japan and South Korea are wealthy, energy-efficient economies with large strategic reserves, and even they are feeling serious pain. Japan's Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry reported that the country's oil reserves stood at 210 days of import cover as of early May, the highest level among IEA members. That buffer has bought Japan time. But it cannot last indefinitely. Japan has been drawing down its reserves at roughly 400,000 barrels per day since mid-April to compensate for the loss of Middle Eastern supply. At that rate, the 210-day buffer shrinks by about two days for every week the crisis continues.</p>

<p>Japan and South Korea have also established a bilateral energy cooperation agreement, the first of its kind between the two nations. Under the arrangement, the two countries share information on available LNG cargo shipments and coordinate strategic reserve withdrawals. This is diplomatically significant: Japan and South Korea have historically been reluctant to cooperate on energy security due to political tensions dating back to Japan's colonial rule of Korea. The fact that they are doing so now tells you how dire the situation is.</p>

<p>South Korea's pain is concentrated in the petrochemical sector. The country is the world's fourth-largest petrochemical producer, and its feedstock comes almost entirely from Middle Eastern crude. Yeocheong Petrochemical, one of Korea's largest producers, announced on May 15 that it was cutting production by 30 percent due to feedstock shortages. The downstream effects on Korea's manufacturing sector are just beginning to show up in the data.</p>

<h2>Europe</h2>

<p>The European Union was already grappling with an energy security crisis following Russia's invasion of Ukraine. The Hormuz crisis has made a bad situation worse. Europe's LNG import terminals are running at maximum capacity, as the Financial Times reported on May 25, but maximum capacity is not enough. Europe needs roughly 350 million cubic meters of LNG per day to meet demand. It can import about 310 million cubic meters per day through its existing terminals. The shortfall is being covered by demand destruction: industrial users in Germany and Italy have curtailed operations, and some smaller manufacturers have shut down entirely.</p>

<p>German industrial production fell 3.1 percent month-over-month in April, the sharpest decline since the early days of the COVID pandemic. The German Chamber of Commerce and Industry, or DIHK, surveyed 2,800 companies in mid-May and found that 41 percent reported significant negative effects from higher energy costs, and 12 percent said they were considering relocating production outside of Germany.</p>

<h2>The United States</h2>

<p>The US economy is the least affected among major nations, for obvious reasons. The United States is a net oil exporter. Domestic production has actually increased by roughly 400,000 barrels per day since the crisis began, as shale drillers in the Permian Basin accelerated completion of drilled but uncompleted wells, or DUCs. The national average gasoline price hit $4.12 per gallon on May 16, which is painful for consumers but well below the $5.02 peak of June 2022.</p>

<p>The GDP impact is real but modest. The IMF's 0.5 percentage point downgrade for the US reflects higher input costs for energy-intensive industries, reduced consumer spending on non-energy goods due to higher fuel costs, and the fiscal cost of the strategic reserve drawdown. The SPR released 18 million barrels in April and another 22 million in May. These are not free: the Treasury has to finance the replacement purchases when the crisis ends, adding to the federal deficit.</p>

<h2>The oil company paradox</h2>

<p>While the world pays the price, oil companies are posting record profits. ExxonMobil reported first-quarter 2026 earnings of $12.4 billion, up 47 percent from the same period in 2025. Chevron's earnings hit $8.9 billion. Saudi Aramco's net income for the quarter was $33.2 billion. Shell, BP, and TotalEnergies all posted results that exceeded analyst expectations by wide margins.</p>

<p>This is not a coincidence or an accident. It is how the commodity market works. When supply is constrained and demand is relatively inelastic, prices rise. Producers with access to supply capture the difference as profit. The fact that the supply constraint is caused by a geopolitical crisis rather than a deliberate production cut does not change the economics. If anything, it makes the profit windfall more politically toxic.</p>

<p>Senator Elizabeth Warren introduced a bill on May 18 that would impose a 50 percent surtax on oil company profits above a baseline calculated from the 2023-2024 average. The bill is unlikely to pass in its current form, but it reflects a growing political anger that is not going away. When gasoline costs $4.12 per gallon and Exxon makes $12.4 billion in three months, people notice. They notice especially in developing countries where fuel costs are a much larger share of household income.</p>

<h2>The inequality at the center of this crisis</h2>

<p>The $1 trillion cost is not distributed evenly. It never is. The IMF's data shows that low-income countries, which account for about 10 percent of global GDP, are absorbing roughly 25 percent of the economic damage. This is because energy costs represent a much larger share of their economies, they have no strategic reserves to draw down, they lack the fiscal space to subsidize fuel, and their currencies are weaker, which amplifies the dollar-denominated increase in oil prices.</p>

<p>The World Bank estimates that the crisis could push an additional 40 million people below the extreme poverty line, defined as living on less than $2.15 per day. Most of these people are in Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia. They will never see an oil company profit statement. They will experience this crisis as higher food prices, power outages, lost jobs, and the quiet devastation of being unable to afford basic necessities.</p>

<p>The IEA expects the oil supply disruption to persist into 2027. If that forecast holds, the $1 trillion number will look conservative. The cumulative cost over two years could easily exceed $2 trillion. And the distribution of that cost, with the poorest countries paying the highest price relative to their means, will be the lasting legacy of this crisis long after the tankers start moving through Hormuz again.</p>
