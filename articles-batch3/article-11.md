<h2>Ninety days of policy chaos</h2>

<p>If you are an oil trader trying to figure out whether you can legally buy Iranian crude this week, I do not envy you. Between February 6 and May 11 of this year, the United States government sanctioned Iranian oil, lifted some of those sanctions, and then sanctioned it again. The whole cycle took roughly 90 days. Markets are dizzy. Refiners are sitting on cargo they cannot legally move. And the State Department's public statements have done almost nothing to clear up the confusion.</p>

<p>I have been tracking sanctions policy for twelve years, and I cannot recall a sequence this disorganized. The Obama Iran sanctions were complex but consistent in direction: pressure, then more pressure, then the JCPOA relief. The first Trump administration's "maximum pressure" campaign was brutal but at least predictable. This time around, the administration seems to be making it up as it goes, reacting to oil prices one week and to hawkish senators the next.</p>

<p>What follows is a detailed timeline of the flip-flop, what each action actually did, and what it means for the companies caught in the middle.</p>

<h2>February 6: the opening salvo</h2>

<p>The first move came on February 6, when the Treasury Department's Office of Foreign Assets Control, or OFAC, designated fifteen entities connected to what it called Iran's "shadow fleet" operations. These were not the usual suspects. The designations hit a network of shipping companies registered in the Marshall Islands, two vessel management firms in Dubai, and a commodities trader based in Hong Kong that had been facilitating ship-to-ship transfers of Iranian crude in the waters off Malaysia and Singapore.</p>

<p>The specific mechanism here matters. OFAC's designations freeze any US-linked assets belonging to these entities and make it a sanctionable offense for any person or company to do business with them. In practice, this meant that any bank processing payments connected to these firms could face secondary sanctions. The shipping insurance market reacted immediately. Lloyd's Syndicate and several Protection and Indemnity clubs, or P&I clubs, pulled coverage from vessels associated with the named entities within 48 hours.</p>

<p>At the time, the sanctions looked like a continuation of maximum pressure. The language in the Treasury press release was standard: "Iran's illicit oil trade funds terrorism and destabilizing activities." Secretary of State Marco Rubio echoed the same line in a press conference that afternoon. Nobody in the market expected what would come six weeks later.</p>

<h2>March 20: the waiver nobody saw coming</h2>

<p>On March 20, the State Department announced a temporary sanctions waiver for Iranian oil already at sea. The waiver, issued under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act, allowed foreign purchasers to buy Iranian crude that was already loaded onto tankers and floating in international waters. The stated reason was straightforward: gasoline prices had hit $3.89 per gallon nationally, and the administration was feeling the political heat.</p>

<p>The waiver was extraordinary in both its scope and its timing. It applied to an estimated 35 million barrels of Iranian crude sitting on floating storage in the Strait of Hormuz, off the coast of Fujairah, and in the South China Sea. The Treasury guidance specified that the waiver covered purchases made between March 20 and April 19, a thirty-day window. Any oil that had not yet been loaded was not covered. Any new contracts for future delivery were not covered. It was a narrow, temporary breach in the sanctions wall.</p>

<p>The market reaction was immediate and chaotic. Brent crude dropped $4.20 in the hour after the announcement, then partially recovered as traders realized the volume was limited. Chinese independent refiners, known as teapots, scrambled to buy the floating cargo at discounts of $8 to $12 per barrel below spot. Indian refiners, who had been quietly importing Iranian crude through informal channels anyway, suddenly found they could do so openly for a month.</p>

<p>I remember reading the waiver document and having to go through it twice. The legal construction was unusual. Rather than issuing a general license through OFAC, which is the normal mechanism, the State Department used its own authority under IEEPA to issue the waiver directly. This bypassed the usual interagency review process at Treasury and Commerce. Several Treasury officials I spoke with, speaking on background because they were not authorized to discuss internal deliberations, said they learned about the waiver from the press release.</p>

<h2>The Senate fights back</h2>

<p>The backlash from Capitol Hill was swift and bipartisan. Senator Jim Risch, the Republican chairman of the Senate Foreign Relations Committee, called the waiver "a $14 billion windfall for the Iranian regime" during a hearing on March 25. His math went like this: 35 million barrels at roughly $85 per barrel comes to just under $3 billion in direct revenue, but the secondary effects, including the lifting of insurance restrictions and the normalization of Iranian oil transactions even for a limited window, would funnel closer to $14 billion into Iranian government accounts when you accounted for restored banking relationships and the knock-on effect on other commodity trades.</p>

<p>Whether the $14 billion figure is accurate depends on how generously you count knock-on effects. The direct revenue from the floating oil was probably closer to $2.5 to $3 billion. But Risch had a point about the broader normalization. Once banks processed a single legal Iranian oil payment, the compliance infrastructure for future payments was already in place. You cannot easily put that toothpaste back in the tube.</p>

<p>Senator Bob Menendez, the ranking Democrat on the same committee, was equally harsh. He pointed out that the administration had not consulted Congress before issuing the waiver, which he argued violated the spirit if not the letter of the Iran Sanctions Act. Menendez introduced a resolution on March 27 to overturn the waiver. It failed to pass, but the vote was closer than the White House expected: 48 to 52.</p>

<h2>April 19: the waiver expires</h2>

<p>The waiver expired on April 19 as scheduled. The State Department did not extend it. By this point, the political calculus had shifted. Gasoline prices had come down slightly, to $3.74 per gallon, partly because the waiver had worked as intended. But the political cost was enormous. Every Republican senator and a dozen Democrats had publicly criticized the move. The House was preparing its own disapproval resolution.</p>

<p>The expiration created its own set of problems. Several tankers that had loaded Iranian crude during the waiver window were still in transit. Their cargoes were now legal, because the purchase had occurred during the waiver period, but the ships themselves were now sailing through waters where Iranian-backed forces were actively threatening commercial traffic. Two of the tankers, the MT Sea Fortune and the MT Pacific Venture, opted to reroute around the Cape of Good Hope rather than transit the Strait of Hormuz, adding 12 days and roughly $800,000 in additional fuel costs per vessel.</p>

<h2>April 24: back to maximum pressure</h2>

<p>Five days after the waiver expired, OFAC came out swinging. On April 24, the Treasury designated eight entities in what it described as the "Iran-China oil pipeline," a loose network of trading companies, shipping firms, and refining operations that had been moving Iranian crude to Chinese independent refineries. The designations hit two trading houses in Zhoushan, a petrochemical storage company in Singapore, and three vessel operators with registrations in Panama and Liberia.</p>

<p>This round of sanctions was specifically designed to close the loopholes that the March waiver had inadvertently opened. The Treasury added a new provision: any financial institution that had processed payments for Iranian oil during the waiver period was now required to file a retroactive Suspicious Activity Report if those payments involved any entity subsequently designated. This created a compliance nightmare for banks in China, India, and South Korea that had processed waiver-period transactions in good faith.</p>

<p>The compliance departments at several major banks were, to put it mildly, furious. A compliance officer at Standard Chartered, who spoke on condition of anonymity because they were not authorized to speak publicly, told me their team had spent three weeks building new processing rails for Iranian oil payments under the waiver. Now those same transactions needed to be reviewed and potentially reported as suspicious. "It is like being told the speed limit is 65, you drive 65, and then the police say actually the limit was 55 yesterday and here is your ticket," this person said.</p>

<h2>May 11: twelve more designations</h2>

<p>The most recent round came on May 11, when OFAC designated twelve additional entities. This time the focus was on vessel management companies operating out of the UAE and Oman that had been arranging flag changes and document falsification for tankers carrying Iranian crude. Three of the designated firms were registered in Fujairah Free Zone, which has long been a hub for ship-to-ship transfers and offshore storage.</p>

<p>One of the designated companies, Al Fajr Maritime Services LLC, had been operating openly in Fujairah for years and was a registered member of the local shipping association. Its designation sent a chill through the entire Fujairah bunkering market. Several other firms in the free zone voluntarily suspended operations pending legal review, according to a notice from the Fujairah Maritime Authority dated May 13.</p>

<h2>What this means for the oil market</h2>

<p>The policy whiplash has created a genuine problem for anyone trying to plan around Iranian oil. Before the waiver, the calculus was simple: Iranian oil was sanctioned, full stop. Refiners who wanted it had to operate in the shadows. After the waiver, a brief window of legality opened. Now that window has closed again, but the market structure it briefly created has not fully undone itself.</p>

<p>Consider what happened to the spread between Brent and Dubai crude. Before February 6, the Dubai benchmark traded at a discount of roughly $2.50 per barrel to Brent, reflecting the availability of Iranian and other Middle Eastern crude. After the February sanctions, the discount widened to $4.10 as Chinese teapots pulled back. After the March waiver, it narrowed back to $2.80. After the April 24 sanctions, it widened again to $3.90. As of this writing, it sits at $4.30. The volatility in this spread alone has cost refiners in Asia hundreds of millions of dollars in hedging losses.</p>

<p>Indian refiners are particularly exposed. Reliance Industries, which operates the world's largest refinery complex at Jamnagar, had imported roughly 180,000 barrels per day of Iranian crude through informal channels in January and February. After the March waiver, those imports became briefly legitimate, and Reliance increased them to 240,000 barrels per day. After the April sanctions, the company had to scramble to find alternative supplies from Iraq and Saudi Arabia at higher prices. A Reliance spokesperson declined to comment for this article.</p>

<h2>The compliance industry is swamped</h2>

<p>The sanctions flip-flop has been a bonanza for law firms and compliance consultants, and a headache for everyone else. K2 Integrity, one of the leading sanctions advisory firms, told me they received more than 200 client inquiries in the 48 hours after the March waiver was announced. Many of those same clients called back in April asking whether their now-legal transactions had become illegal again.</p>

<p>The cost of compliance is not trivial. A mid-sized trading company can expect to spend $200,000 to $500,000 on legal fees for a single sanctions review. For companies that traded Iranian oil during the waiver window, the review now has to cover both the original transaction and its post-waiver status. "We are basically doing the same work twice," a partner at a Washington law firm told me. "The first time to tell clients they can do the deal. The second time to tell them they might need to unwind it."</p>

<h2>The bigger picture</h2>

<p>The 90-day flip-flop reveals something deeper about how sanctions policy is being made in this administration. The February sanctions were driven by national security officials at the NSC and State. The March waiver was driven by domestic political concerns about gasoline prices. The April and May sanctions were driven by congressional pressure. There is no coherent overarching strategy. Each move is a reaction to the last crisis, not a step toward a defined goal.</p>

<p>For countries trying to navigate US sanctions, this is worse than a consistently hard line. At least with consistent maximum pressure, you know where you stand. With this back-and-forth, you might buy oil legally on Tuesday and find out on Thursday that the same transaction is now suspect. The result is not compliance. It is paralysis. And in a market that is already short 8 percent of global supply because of the Hormuz crisis, paralysis is the last thing anyone needs.</p>

<p>The Senate Foreign Relations Committee has scheduled hearings for June 3 on the administration's Iran sanctions policy. Expect sharp questions about the waiver, the $14 billion figure, and the process by which these decisions are being made. Whether those hearings produce any clarity remains to be seen. I am not holding my breath.</p>
