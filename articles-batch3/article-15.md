<h2>Keeping count</h2>

<p>I started maintaining a running log of maritime incidents in the Strait of Hormuz and the broader Persian Gulf in early February. At the time, the list was short: a couple of suspicious approaches, some radio threats, the kind of harassment that has been routine in those waters for years. By late April, the log had become something else entirely. Ships were being seized. Shots were being fired. The waterway that carries roughly 20 percent of the world's oil was turning into an active conflict zone.</p>

<p>What follows is a complete chronology of every confirmed seizure, attack, and armed approach involving commercial vessels in the Strait of Hormuz and adjacent waters since February 1, 2026. I have verified each incident through at least two independent sources: press reports, government statements, ship tracking data, or direct communication with ship operators or insurance markets. Where details are uncertain, I say so. Where I have only a single source, I note that too. This is a living document, and I will update it as new information becomes available.</p>

<h2>February 12: first armed approach</h2>

<p>On February 12, the Marshall Islands-flagged product tanker MT Oceanic Grace reported that two small craft, described as "fast boats with armed personnel," approached to within 300 meters while the vessel was transiting the Strait of Hormuz outbound. The crew observed weapons, including what appeared to be heavy machine guns mounted on the bows of the approaching boats. The boats shadowed the Oceanic Grace for approximately 18 minutes before breaking off and heading north toward Iranian territorial waters.</p>

<p>The Oceanic Grace's master reported the incident to the UK Maritime Trade Operations, or UKMTO, which issued an alert to shipping in the area. No shots were fired. No boarding was attempted. At the time, the incident was treated as an escalation of the routine IRGC harassment that has been common in the strait for years. Looking back, it was the opening move of a sustained campaign.</p>

<h2>February 26: second armed approach</h2>

<p>Two weeks later, on February 26, a similar incident occurred. The Liberian-flagged crude oil tanker MT Nordic Pioneer reported that three fast boats made a coordinated approach while the vessel was in the inbound traffic lane, approximately 15 nautical miles southwest of Qeshm Island. The boats came within 200 meters and trained what the crew described as a laser designator on the ship's bridge. The master increased speed to 16 knots and altered course toward the Omani side of the strait. The boats pursued for approximately 25 minutes before turning away.</p>

<p>A laser designator is not a weapon in itself, but it is used to guide munitions to a target. The implication of pointing one at a commercial vessel's bridge is unmistakable: we can hit you if we choose to. The Nordic Pioneer's operating company, Nordic Shipholding, confirmed the incident in a statement but declined to speculate on the identity of the boats. The IRGC has not commented on either the February 12 or February 26 incidents.</p>

<h2>March 8: first attempted seizure</h2>

<p>On March 8, the situation escalated from harassment to attempted seizure. The Panamanian-flagged bulk carrier MV Starlight was transiting the strait outbound when four IRGCN fast boats closed on the vessel at high speed. The boats attempted to force the Starlight to alter course toward Iranian waters. The Starlight's master radioed a distress call on Channel 16, which was received by the USS Carney, then on patrol in the southern strait.</p>

<p>The Carney responded immediately, closing the distance at flank speed and launching its MH-60R helicopter to provide overhead surveillance. The helicopter crew reported that the Iranian boats were attempting to grapple the Starlight's hull. The Carney's commanding officer issued a radio warning and trained the destroyer's main battery on the approaching boats. The IRGCN craft broke off and withdrew. The entire engagement lasted approximately 35 minutes.</p>

<p>The Pentagon confirmed the incident in a press statement on March 9, describing it as "an attempted unlawful seizure of a commercial vessel in international waters." Iran's mission to the United Nations denied any seizure attempt, claiming the boats were conducting "routine maritime security patrols" and that the Starlight had been in Iranian territorial waters. Vessel tracking data from MarineTraffic, reviewed by Reuters, showed the Starlight was in the internationally recognized traffic separation scheme, well outside Iranian territorial waters, at the time of the approach.</p>

<h2>March 29: armed approach on LNG carrier</h2>

<p>On March 29, the Malta-flagged LNG carrier MV Al Gharrafa, operated by Nakilat Shipping, a Qatari state-owned company, reported an approach by two armed fast boats while transiting the strait outbound with a full cargo of LNG. The boats came within 400 meters and trained weapons on the vessel but did not attempt to board. The Al Gharrafa's master increased speed and maintained course. The boats broke off after approximately 12 minutes.</p>

<p>This incident was notable because of the target. An LNG carrier is a floating bomb. A single round penetrating a cargo tank could trigger a catastrophic chain reaction. The IRGCN knows this. Approaching an LNG carrier with weapons visible is a form of intimidation that carries an implicit threat of mass destruction. QatarEnergy subsequently issued an internal directive, reported by Lloyd's List on April 2, instructing all Nakilat vessels to avoid transiting the strait without naval escort.</p>

<h2>April 10: second attempted seizure</h2>

<p>On April 10, US naval forces prevented a second attempted seizure. The details are sketchier than the March 8 incident because the Pentagon's public statement was more restrained, referring only to "US forces intervening to ensure the safety of a commercial vessel in the Strait of Hormuz." But two Defense Department officials, speaking on condition of anonymity, confirmed that IRGCN fast boats attempted to board a Greek-flagged crude oil tanker, the MT Hellas Voyager, and were driven off by a US Navy patrol craft that placed itself between the Iranian boats and the tanker.</p>

<p>The Hellas Voyager was carrying approximately 1 million barrels of crude oil from Iraq's Basrah terminal to Greece. The vessel's manager, Eastern Mediterranean Shipping, confirmed the incident but declined to provide details, citing "ongoing security concerns." The company has since rerouted all its Gulf-bound vessels around the Cape of Good Hope, adding approximately $1.2 million in additional costs per voyage.</p>

<h2>April 19: IRGC fires on a tanker</h2>

<p>This was the moment the conflict shifted from harassment and attempted seizures to actual violence. On April 19, IRGC gunboats fired on the Marshall Islands-flagged chemical tanker MT Aqua Fortune while it was transiting the strait inbound. The vessel was hit by small-arms fire on its port side, with rounds striking the accommodation block and damaging a lifeboat. No crew members were injured. The Aqua Fortune's master reported the attack to UKMTO and continued his transit.</p>

<p>IRGC spokesman Ramazan Sharif confirmed the engagement in a statement on Iranian state television, claiming the Aqua Fortune had violated Iranian maritime regulations and was "carrying contraband." No evidence was provided for the contraband claim. The Aqua Fortune's operator, Aegean Shipping Management of Athens, rejected the allegation and said the vessel was carrying a legitimate cargo of naphtha from the UAE to India.</p>

<p>The shooting represented a significant escalation. Until April 19, the IRGCN had used weapons only for intimidation: pointing them, training lasers, firing warning shots. Actually hitting a commercial vessel with live ammunition crossed a line that had not been crossed since the Tanker War of the 1980s. The insurance market responded within hours. The Joint War Committee of Lloyd's Market Association expanded its listed areas of heightened risk to include the entire Strait of Hormuz and the Persian Gulf west of 56 degrees East longitude. War risk premiums for transits through the area tripled overnight.</p>

<h2>April 22: Iran seizes two cargo ships</h2>

<p>Three days after the shooting, Iran seized two vessels. On the morning of April 22, IRGCN forces boarded and took control of the Portuguese-flagged general cargo ship MSC Aries and the Liberian-flagged bulk carrier MV Good Hope. Both vessels were diverted to Iranian ports. The MSC Aries was taken to Bandar Abbas. The MV Good Hope was taken to the port of Chabahar, on the Gulf of Oman outside the strait.</p>

<p>The MSC Aries is operated by Zodiac Maritime, a company controlled by the Israeli Ofer family. Iran has not hidden its motive for this seizure. IRGC Commander Hossein Salami stated on state television that the vessel was seized "in retaliation for the Zionist regime's aggression against Iranian interests." The MSC Aries has a crew of 22, predominantly Filipino and Indian nationals. As of this writing, the crew remains on board the vessel at anchor in Bandar Abbas. The Philippine Department of Migrant Workers has confirmed that 14 Filipino crew members are among those detained and has demanded their release through diplomatic channels.</p>

<p>The MV Good Hope's seizure has been more mysterious. The vessel is owned by a Liberian-registered company with no apparent connection to Israel or to any party in the regional conflict. Iran has not publicly explained why this vessel was seized. A source at the International Maritime Organization, who spoke on condition of anonymity because the organization's diplomatic processes are confidential, told me the working assumption is that the Good Hope was seized as leverage, a bargaining chip to be traded later in negotiations. The vessel has a crew of 19, including 12 Indian nationals. India's Ministry of External Affairs has confirmed it is "engaged with Iranian authorities" regarding the crew.</p>

<h2>April 22: US seizes Iranian-linked tanker</h2>

<p>On the same day Iran seized two ships, the United States seized one of its own. The Associated Press confirmed that US forces seized an Iranian-associated tanker in the Gulf of Oman, east of the strait. The vessel, which AP identified as the MV Yaz I, was carrying a cargo of Iranian crude believed to be destined for a Chinese independent refinery. The seizure was conducted under the authority of the April 24 OFAC sanctions designation, which had been prepared in advance but not yet publicly announced.</p>

<p>The timing was not coincidental. By seizing an Iranian-linked vessel on the same day Iran seized two commercial ships, the United States was sending a message: if you take our ships, we take yours. The MV Yaz I's cargo, estimated at 800,000 barrels of crude, was confiscated. The crew, composed entirely of Iranian nationals, was taken into US custody and subsequently repatriated. The legal basis for the seizure, under US sanctions authority, is different from Iran's basis, which is essentially the threat of force. But in practical terms, both actions amount to the same thing: a ship taken, a cargo stolen, a crew held against their will.</p>

<h2>May 3 and May 14: continued harassment</h2>

<p>The armed approaches have not stopped. On May 3, the Singapore-flagged tanker MT Pacific Ruby reported that two fast boats with armed personnel approached to within 250 meters near the eastern entrance to the strait. No shots were fired. The boats shadowed the vessel for 20 minutes before departing. On May 14, a similar approach occurred against the Bahamian-flagged crude carrier MT Stena Supreme. In this incident, the fast boats came within 150 meters and one of them fired a burst from a heavy machine gun into the water off the Stena Supreme's bow, an unmistakable warning shot.</p>

<p>These lower-level incidents receive less media attention than the seizures and shootings, but they are arguably more significant in aggregate. Each armed approach forces the targeted vessel to take evasive action, which costs time and fuel. Each incident forces other vessels in the area to alter course, creating a ripple effect that disrupts the traffic separation scheme. And each occurrence erodes the willingness of shipping companies to send their vessels into the Gulf at all.</p>

<h2>The insurance fallout</h2>

<p>The cumulative effect of these incidents on the marine insurance market has been severe. The International Group of P&I Clubs, which provides liability coverage for roughly 90 percent of the world's ocean-going tonnage, has imposed additional premiums for Gulf transits that range from 0.5 to 2 percent of the vessel's insured value, depending on the type of cargo and the vessel's flag. For a large crude carrier valued at $80 million, that means an additional $400,000 to $1.6 million per transit.</p>

<p>Some P&I clubs have gone further. Skuld, a Norwegian P&I club, announced on May 8 that it would no longer provide war risk coverage for any vessel transiting the Strait of Hormuz. Shipowners who are members of Skuld must now purchase standalone war risk policies from specialist underwriters at Lloyd's, at rates that have increased roughly tenfold since January. Other clubs are expected to follow Skuld's lead if the incidents continue.</p>

<p>The Guardian reported on May 24 that cumulative insurance claims from the Hormuz crisis have exceeded $2 billion. That figure includes hull and machinery damage from the April 19 shooting, cargo losses from the seized vessels, and business interruption claims from ships that have been unable to complete their voyages. The claims are still being processed, and the final total will likely be much higher once the fate of the three detained vessels and their cargoes is resolved.</p>

<h2>Three ships still held</h2>

<p>As of this writing, three vessels remain in Iranian custody: the MSC Aries, seized April 22 and held at Bandar Abbas; the MV Good Hope, seized April 22 and held at Chabahar; and the MT Asia Union, a small product tanker that was seized in a separate incident on April 28 that I have not yet detailed because information has been slow to emerge. The Asia Union, flagged in Palau, was boarded by IRGCN personnel while at anchor near Qeshm Island. The boarding was reported by UKMTO on April 28, and the vessel's AIS transponder was turned off shortly thereafter. The ship's operator, a company registered in Hong Kong, has not responded to requests for comment.</p>

<p>Seventy-nine seafarers are currently detained on these three vessels. Most are Filipino, Indian, or Vietnamese nationals. The International Transport Workers' Federation has called their detention "a gross violation of international maritime law and basic human rights." Negotiations for their release are ongoing through multiple diplomatic channels, but progress has been slow. In the meantime, the crews are effectively hostages. They cannot leave their ships. Their families are frantic. And the waterways where they were seized grow more dangerous by the day.</p>
